# Riverknoll Renewal research library

Draft for review, November 26 2026. Concept research by the initiative organizer and collaborators. Not an approved RIT project, bid or permit set. Every figure is an estimate from the feasibility model unless it says otherwise, and each section names where to check it.

Latest working files: feasibility model v24.2 (System 3E stacked at the maker's speed) with its wood-frame comparison plan, material list v2, the System 3E vs wood frame life cycle v2 and pitch deck v28 with the model v24.3 numbers (November 26 2026). Every earlier version is listed in version-history.md.

## 1. What happens to Riverknoll

- RIT has announced that Riverknoll closes permanently after the 2026–27 academic year. No demolition or replacement plan was stated (RIT Reporter, Nov 9 2026: https://reporter.rit.edu/8904/showcase/riverknoll-to-be-permanently-closed-after-2027-academic-year/).
- RIT's 2022 campus master plan anticipated a new residence hall that would allow Riverknoll to be retired (RIT News, Dec 5 2022: https://www.rit.edu/news/rit-readies-campus-master-plan-will-guide-future-growth-and-development).
- RIT's October 2026 capital budget has no Riverknoll line; October 2024 listed $990,000 of foundation repairs (https://www.rit.edu/fa/sites/rit.edu.fa/files/October%202026%20Capital%20Listing.pdf).
- Built 1970 as modular wood-frame apartments and townhouses on about 14.5 acres with 328 parking spaces (DASNY/RIT 2019 SEQR: https://www.dasny.org/sites/default/files/inline-files/RIT%202019%20Financing%20Type%20I%2009.10.19%20SES.pdf).

Working assumption: RIT's decommissioning pays for demolition and asbestos abatement. Our estimate of that cost is kept visible, outside this budget: about $12.7M at today's prices, about $22.6M all in (model, Value_Engineering Parts G and H).

## 2. How many beds

The plan is demand-led at 744 new beds, with a sourced range of 444 to 871 from RIT's Common Data Sets and housing figures (model, Demand tab). The historic 513-bed count and the 444 beds in RIT's 2022 Campus Plan still need confirming against RIT's current inventory.

## 3. Design

- Built in System 3E from Poland: dry-stacked perlite blocks for the exterior walls (352 mm, about 13.9 in, published R-27.8), the walls between townhomes and the inside partitions (System 3E INTERNAL 115), plastered inside and finished outside with a thin render. Floors over other floors are precast concrete plank on concrete ring beams; only the roof trusses stay wood (System 3E technical page: https://system3e.us/technology.html). System 3E is not a panel product: an earlier version (v22) wrongly called it prefab facade panels.
- Fire: System 3E's 352 mm load-bearing wall lasted 244 minutes in a European fire test (EN 1365-1, classed REI 240). NY townhouse walls need 1 or 2 hours tested to the US standard (ASTM E119 or UL 263, IRC R302.2), so a code official still has to accept the European test, or the wall needs a US test.
- Sound: System 3E gives about Rw 45 dB for its wall; walls between homes are usually held to STC 50, so the party wall may need a second layer (covered in the high cost case).
- Local sourcing rule: everything except the System 3E blocks comes from suppliers within a couple of miles of Riverknoll. The material list names the nearest supplier for each trade; most are within about 3 miles, and the roof truss plant (about 18 miles) and precast plank plant (about 53 miles) are the exceptions, with closer fallbacks (site-built rafters, floors poured on site with ready-mix from about 1.4 miles away).
- 18 identical townhome rows of 10 units, 12 two-story and 6 three-story; a compact 25 x 32 ft four-bed plan (400 GSF a bed); 72 accessible ground-floor beds against 38 required.
- Townhouse rules (IRC R202 and R302.2): side by side, foundation to roof, open on two sides, at most 3 stories; no plumbing in party walls.
- Fire access: a road within 150 ft of every first-floor wall, so a 250 ft row works with a lane on one side; longer rows need lanes on both sides or an approved extension (confirm in the 2025 NY Fire Code).
- New townhouses on public water need sprinklers, and new low-rise buildings must be all-electric in New York (NY All-Electric Buildings Act).

## 4. Cost

- Base (v24.2, same System 3E plan as v24.1): $173.7M total, $233,442 a new bed, about $294 a sq ft for the buildings alone and about $575 a sq ft all in across the project ($299 and $584 for a two-story row), after RIT's decommissioning takes demolition and asbestos off this budget. v24.1 is v24 with System 3E's blocks stacked as fast as the maker says (its FAQ: 145 square meters of wall in 1 day instead of 14), which the organizer chose to assume on Nov 26. Model v24 was $177.0M ($237,854 a bed, $305 and $595 a sq ft for a two-story row); v23 was $158.4M ($212,839 a bed).
- Wood-frame plan, kept for comparison: the same model with 2x6 wood-frame walls, wood party walls and partitions, wood floor joists and advanced framing comes to $167.6M, $225,240 a bed, about $284 a sq ft for the buildings and $555 all in ($288 and $564 for a two-story row). System 3E costs about $6.1M (about $8,200 a bed) more up front; its life-cycle case is in section 4a.
- Were the walls counted twice? No. Walls were always counted once, inside the $300 a sq ft building price, as wood-frame walls. v22 then credited a wrong 5% prefab-panel saving and v23 took wall insulation out, but System 3E's own cost was never added. v24 swaps each wood part for its System 3E part: exterior wall about $23.50 a sq ft of wall installed (blocks, ring beams and lintels, dry-stack labor, render) against about $22.50 for the framed wall with siding; party wall $25.50 against $23; partitions $12.92 against $10.25; plank floors $21 a sq ft against $8 for wood joists. At ordinary block-laying speed that was about $9.4M more than conventional framing (v24); at the maker's stacking speed it is about $6.1M more (v24.1). v24.2 adds the R-5 continuous insulation outside the sheathing that NY's 2025 energy code needs on a wood wall (an R-20 cavity wall alone is about U-0.060 against the U-0.045 limit), about $1.75 a sq ft of wall; against that code-compliant wood building System 3E costs about $5.6M more ($7,500 a bed). The wood plan comes to $168.1M, $225,948 a bed, $285 a sq ft for the buildings and $557 all in. v24.2 also sets 4 closets per home, as material list v2 and the bedroom egress check have; closets sit inside the per square foot price, so the totals do not move.
- None of these are System 3E quotes: System 3E prices each project and publishes no US price. With every part at its low or high end the total is $162.5M ($218k a bed) to $198.8M ($267k a bed). Wood floor joists instead of plank would bring it to $169.8M ($228,267 a bed); floors poured on site are an option not yet priced. System 3E's own online calculator gives about $154 a sq ft for a simple 1,600 sq ft house from European data, without site, permits or US labor.
- Material list check (v2, matched to the design: every material the drawings need, nothing more, including the 3-story homes v1 missed): about $162 a sq ft to build at mid ($97 to $258) and about $320 a sq ft all in, $130,049 a bed, from national installed list prices. That sits near national benchmarks ($162 NAHB, $176 ICC). The model keeps the conservative, institutional figure ($294 a sq ft in v24.1 and v24.2) until a Rochester contractor prices it.
- Next levers: a contractor price near $250 a sq ft or peer-sized homes ($145.9M, $196k a bed); P3 phasing (pilot, then two phases of 8) saves about $7.9M but needs much more money in a single year.
- From v19 to v20.1: compact plan −$44.6M; reused roads and parking −$6.1M; one drawing set −$7.5M; advanced framing −$2.4M; pilot then 4 rows a year +$12.2M; RIT's decommissioning −$22.7M.
- Utilities: inspect and line old sewer mains, replace what fails; new laterals, water services and electric service are needed either way. Flow rises about 45% at 744 beds, so the sewer and electric capacity must be checked with RIT Facilities, MCWA, Henrietta Sewer District and RG&E.
- Asbestos: certified survey before demolition and licensed abatement under NY Industrial Code Rule 56 (https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/title-12/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-56; NYS DOL P231: https://dol.ny.gov/new-york-state-asbestos-survey-requirements-building-permit-applicants-p231).
- Salvage saves almost nothing once removal labor is counted; donating to the Habitat ReStore in Henrietta is an option.

## 4a. System 3E vs wood frame: does System 3E pay for itself?

Asked by the organizer on Nov 26: does System 3E earn back its higher price through less upkeep, less mold and damage, and faster dry-stack building by less skilled crews? Worked out in the life-cycle file (System 3E vs wood frame life cycle v2) and the 3E_vs_Wood_Lifecycle tab of models v24.1 and v24.2. Each case is per bed, all in, in 2026 dollars.

| | System 3E (base) | Wood frame (comparison) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total and per bed | $173.7M, $233,442 | $167.6M, $225,240 |
| Cost per sq ft, buildings / all in | $294 / $575 | $284 / $555 |
| Wall labor hours, all 744 beds | About 49,800 | About 62,900 |
| Crew skill | Dry stack by trained general labor; ring beams, render and plaster still need trades | Framing carpenters |
| Schedule | About 1.3 weeks faster per phase; openings stay each fall, so no semester is gained | Base calendar |
| Walls only: extra upfront cost | About $2,600 a bed | — |
| Whole building with plank floors: extra upfront cost | About $7,800 a bed | — |
| Yearly savings | About $133 a bed (insurance $74, refinishing $28, wall holes $17, energy $9, hidden wet walls $6) | — |
| Walls only: pays back | About year 28 at 5.15%; worth about +$1,300 a bed over 50 years (+$3,700 at 3%) | — |
| Whole building: pays back | Not within 50 years at 5.15% (about −$3,900 a bed; −$1,400 at 3%) | — |

- Faster, less skilled labor is real: about 13,100 fewer wall labor hours (about $617,000 at Rochester construction wages, BLS May 2025, $32.46 an hour before benefits). It is counted once, inside the installed price and the shorter schedule.
- More than half the yearly saving is insurance, taken at half the gap in a concrete-industry survey (NRMCA and Boston College, 2024). If RIT's campus insurance doesn't price buildings by wall type, the saving drops to about $59 a bed a year and the walls never pay back.
- The "idiot proof" point holds but is small in dollars: plaster on block shrugs off the wall holes and hidden wet walls that drywall gets (about $23 a bed a year). What students wear out most (paint, floors, doors, bathrooms, surface mold from showers) costs the same either way.
- Risk: if the first US crews are no faster than ordinary block laying, System 3E walls run about $13,800 a bed behind and never catch up.
- What would settle it: a System 3E USA quote on the drawings (each $1 a sq ft of wall moves the result about $660 a bed), a crew productivity guarantee, and an RIT insurance quote by construction type. Donated or discounted pilot blocks would make the walls cheaper from day one.
- Sources read: System 3E FAQ; Lanthier-Veilleux et al., dampness and mold among university students (IJERPH 2016); HomeGuide drywall repair, mold removal and block wall costs; Loyola University Maryland and Fordham University room damage charges; BLS Rochester wages (May 2025); RSMeans for BASF time and motion study (2006); InterNACHI life expectancy chart; NRMCA and Boston College multifamily insurance survey (2024); Multihousing News on student housing turns; Insulation Institute N112 (NY 2025 code); Graduate School USA on general conditions.
- Version 1 of this comparison (ordinary laying speed, no advanced framing on the wood side) stays on this page for checking.

## 4b. Walls compared three ways, and timber upper floors (v24.3)

Asked by the organizer on Nov 26: put the walls side by side in plain dollars, System 3E against 2x6 wood framing and prefab wood panels, and build on a concrete slab with vinyl plank and timber upper floors. Worked out in walls compared v1 and model v24.3. All in, 744 beds, 2026 dollars.

| Outside walls only | System 3E from Poland today | System 3E, future US plant | 2x6 stick + R-5 | Prefab panels |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Per sq ft of wall, installed and finished | $35.22 | $20.50 | $24.25 | $25.24 |
| All in, per bed | $14,652 | $8,527 | $10,087 | $10,499 |
| Payback | Not in 50 years | Cheaper from day one | Base | No yearly saving |

| Whole building | System 3E, US plant (base) | System 3E from Poland | 2x6 stick + R-5 | Prefab panels |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Total, per bed | $169.8M, $228,266 | $180.8M, $243,017 | $168.1M, $225,948 | $169.0M, $227,183 |
| Per sq ft, buildings / all in | $288 / $562 | $307 / $599 | $285 / $557 | $286 / $560 |
| Pays back | About year 23 with the insurance saving, never without | Never | Base | No yearly saving |

- Imported blocks: System 3E's European list price works out to about $14.93 a sq ft of wall at EUR/USD 1.0978; with 5% breakage, about 75 m2 of wall per 40 ft container, ocean freight $3,083 (Drewry, Nov 12 2026), port $800, US truck $2,000, broker $60 and a 15% tariff (US-EU framework), they land at about $26.72 a sq ft, against the $12 the model assumes from a future US plant.
- Prefab panels: the nearest panel plant found is Barden Building Products in Middleport NY (about 55 miles by road, our estimate), which ships wall panels without siding or windows. A study of 14 apartment buildings measured panels at about 10% more than stick framing for the framing itself ($18.54 vs $16.79 a sq ft, Dec 2020) and about 39% fewer framing days (882 vs 541 sq ft a day).
- Why the whole System 3E building costs more than its walls: at the US price the outside walls save about $1,560 a bed, block party walls and partitions add about $470, and the wood plan's 1.5% advanced-framing saving (about $3,400 a bed) is lost. The concrete plank floors were the main reason v24.2 never paid back; timber floors remove about $5,200 a bed.
- Insurance is more than half the yearly saving ($74 of $133 a bed). Without it the System 3E building never pays back.
- Sources read: System 3E price list and container offer; Trading Economics EUR/USD; Drewry World Container Index; Thomson Reuters on US-EU tariffs; Kuzdas, prefab wall panel schedule and cost analysis (UW-Stout, 2021); Barden Building Products; IBACOS for ENERGY STAR panelized construction primer (2019); NAHB construction cost survey (2024, via SimplyWise).

## 5. Phasing

Year 1 is design, permits and surveys. A 2-row, 72-bed pilot opens August 2029, then 4 rows a year, last homes August 2033. Year 1 spend is about $6.3M and the largest year about $41.9M. Faster patterns cost less overall but need much more in a single year (model v24, Phase_Plan).

## 6. Funding: who pays, and how little can RIT pay

Who borrows changes RIT's share only a little. Today's Riverknoll rent can repay about $33M to $51M of debt, depending on who borrows, against a $173.7M cost (v24.1 and v24.2).

| Who owns and borrows | RIT net share | Share of cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RIT, DASNY bonds over 30 years (base) | $123.2M | 71% |
| RIT, DASNY bonds over 40 years | $117.8M | 68% |
| Nonprofit owner on an RIT ground lease | $129.5M | 75% |
| Same nonprofit, weaker loan terms | $132.7M | 76% |
| Private developer on a ground lease | $111.9M | 64% |

Outside money: about $10.7M in the base (NYSERDA heat-pump incentive and gifts), up to $38.6M if every source lands high.

- Can qualify: NYSERDA New Efficient Homes heat-pump incentive (https://www.nyserda.ny.gov/All-Programs/New-Efficient-Homes-New-York/Heat-Pump-Incentive); NYSERDA Buildings of Excellence, uncertain for student housing; the federal geothermal heat-pump credit as cash for a tax-exempt owner, only with ground-source heat pumps (IRS elective pay: https://www.irs.gov/node/123396); RG&E ground-source rebate (https://www.rge.com/documents/d/global/building-electrification-rebate-catalog-pdf); HECap, uncertain because residence halls are not named (https://dasny.org/sites/default/files/cta-link-files/2025/HECap%20FAQ.pdf); philanthropy, with RIT's naming policy reserving residence halls for commemorative naming (https://www.rit.edu/giving/sites/rit.edu.giving/files/RIT%20Gift%20Acceptance%20and%20Naming%20Policy_Revised%202025_final.pdf).
- Ruled out: low-income housing tax credits (full-time student rule, IRC 42(i)(3)(D), and the general public use rule, 26 CFR 1.42-9), HUD college housing loans, Empire State Development capital (excludes residential-only projects), NY-Sun upstate commercial, 45L and 179D (ended for construction after June 30 2026: https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/faqs-for-modification-of-sections-25c-25d-25e-30c-30d-45l-45w-and-179d-under-public-law-119-21-139-stat-72-july-4-2025-commonly-kno), and the 48E solar credit (must be in service by end of 2027).
- Precedents for a nonprofit or developer partner on campus land: Lynn University and Provident ($81.6M, 743 beds), URI and Gilbane (about 1,100 beds, $210M tax-exempt bonds, Nov 2025: https://www.uri.edu/news/2025/11/uri-gilbane-reach-financial-close-on-partnership-to-build-and-operate-new-on-campus-student-housing/). Rating agencies count such debt with the university (Moody's higher education methodology), and partner housing debt has been downgraded when occupancy fell (Texas A&M Park West).
- RIT's capacity (FY2026 audited statements: https://www.rit.edu/controller/sites/rit.edu.controller/files/2026-11/FY2026%20RIT%20Financial%20Statements%20Signed.pdf): endowment $1.683B, debt principal $377.7M, liquid assets within a year $464.8M. Borrowing the $132M gap at 5.15% over 30 years would add roughly $9M a year of debt service, about 1% of revenue (our estimate).

What it takes for RIT to pay a minority share:

| Combination | Total cost | RIT net share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Base plan (System 3E blocks), Riverknoll-level rent | $173.7M | $123.2M |
| B. A with peer-sized homes | $145.9M | $95.4M |
| C. A with rent near off-campus (about 1.5x Riverknoll) | $173.7M | $87.5M |
| D. B and C together | $145.9M | $59.7M |

To cap RIT at $50M on the base plan, rent would need about $13,800 a bed-year in today's dollars, more than twice Riverknoll's $6,762 and above APEX's about $10,188. Phasing helps cash flow, not the total: old Riverknoll closes after spring 2027, and each new phase's rent starts repaying its own loan.

The realistic route for the initiative: Projxodus as initiator and advocate; an RIT call for proposals that invites established nonprofit owners and developers; and a student and community advisory seat written into any ground lease. A student or a new nonprofit cannot realistically own or borrow for the project.

## 7. Wider value

- Energy: System 3E's published R-27.8 wall against an assumed R-22 wall saves about $6,600 a year in utilities across the 744 beds (model v24, System3E), counted in the wider value.
- Insulation: on the maker's data the System 3E wall alone (U-0.0345) beats the strictest NY 2025 energy code line for climate zone 5 (U-0.045 for a frame wall) by about 23%, so the plan adds no wall insulation; the code official still has to accept it. Extra insulation beyond code does not pay back: added wall insulation saves about $3,300 a year across all homes for about $686,000. Air sealing with a blower-door test does: about $16,100 a year for about $223,000, roughly 14 years. Triple-pane windows take about 60 years on energy alone (model v24, Insulation_Payback).
- Long run: on rent alone the rebuild repays the gap around 2077 in nominal dollars and never at RIT's borrowing cost; it is just above even (about +$1.8M) once wider benefits are counted (model v24, Long_Run and Total_Value).
- Market: with no rebuild, about 340 more students would rent in Henrietta, pushing local rents up slightly (model, Market_Impact).
