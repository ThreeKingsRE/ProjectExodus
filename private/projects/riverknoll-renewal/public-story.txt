Riverknoll Renewal is a student-led proposal for affordable upperclass housing at RIT. RIT plans to close Riverknoll after the 2026–27 year. This page asks what it would take to rebuild it as affordable, all-electric townhomes, and shows all of our work, every version and every option, so anyone can check it and choose for themselves.

The initiative is independent. RIT has not approved or funded it. Figures are concept research and allowances, not bids. Updated November 26, 2026 (model v24.3 with timber upper floors, walls compared three ways v1, material list v2, deck v27).

The straight answer: System 3E walls win only if the blocks come from a US plant near the model's $12 a sq ft, which is our assumption, not a quote, and no US plant is open yet. Bought at System 3E's European list price and shipped today, they land at about $26.72 a sq ft of wall before install, and the walls cost about $4,600 a bed more than wood with no payback in 50 years. Even at the US price, the whole System 3E building costs about $2,300 a bed more than wood: the outside walls save about $1,560 a bed, but block party walls and partitions cost a little more than wood ones (about +$470 a bed) and the wood plan's advanced-framing saving (about 1.5% of the building, about $3,400 a bed) is lost. More than half of the yearly saving is cheaper insurance for block buildings, which RIT has not confirmed.

Pros and cons, briefly.

System 3E: the wall meets NY code with no added insulation (R-27.8 on the maker's data), has nothing to rot or feed mold, and plaster takes dents and holes better than drywall; trained general labor stacks it fast. But there is no US plant, one maker, freight and tariff risk, a European fire test that a US code official must accept, and ring beams that still need trades.

2x6 wood stick: the cheapest proven way, with local lumber, local crews and inspectors who know it. It needs outside foam, wrap and siding to meet code, drywall dents, and a failed wrap can hide wet walls.

Prefab wood panels: about 39% fewer framing days on site and factory-square walls. They cost about 10% more for framing, come from about 55 miles away (our estimate), which breaks the couple-of-miles sourcing rule, need crane and truck access between rows, and have the same upkeep as stick.

These figures replace the payback figures in life cycle v2, which assumed concrete plank floors. Sources: System 3E price list and container offer; Drewry container index (Nov 12, 2026); Trading Economics EUR/USD (Nov 26, 2026); Thomson Reuters on the US-EU tariff framework; Kuzdas, prefab wall panel cost study (UW-Stout, 2021); Barden Building Products; IBACOS for ENERGY STAR; plus the sources already in the research library.

## Model v24.2: System 3E and wood frame side by side

The newest work comes first; every earlier version stays on this page below. v24.2 keeps the whole building in System 3E and assumes crews stack the blocks as fast as System 3E says (145 square meters of wall in 1 day instead of 14). The same model built in ordinary wood framing stays here as a second plan, so you can compare the two and choose.

| What | System 3E plan (base) | Wood-frame plan (comparison) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Walls and floors | Dry-stacked perlite blocks for outside walls, walls between homes and partitions; concrete plank floors | 2x6 wood framing with insulation plus R-5 insulation outside the sheathing (needed for NY's energy code), wood party walls and partitions, wood floor joists, advanced framing |
| Total | $173.7M | $168.1M |
| Per bed | $233,442 | $225,948 |
| Per sq ft, buildings only | About $294 | About $285 |
| Per sq ft, all in | About $575 | About $557 |
| RIT's share (base funding) | About $123.2M | About $117.6M |
| Wall labor | About 49,800 hours, mostly trained general labor | About 62,900 hours of framing carpentry |
| Schedule | About 1.3 weeks faster per phase; homes still open each fall | Base calendar |
| Upkeep over the years | About $133 a bed a year less (insurance, refinishing, wall holes, energy, hidden wet walls) | Base |

What v24.2 fixed: each home now has 4 closets, matching the material list and the bedroom egress check (no cost change, since closets sit inside the price per square foot). The wood-frame plan now has R-5 insulation outside the sheathing, which NY's energy code needs on a wood wall; that adds about $0.5M, so System 3E now costs about $5.6M ($7,500 a bed) more than a code-compliant wood building. The System 3E plan itself did not change.

Does System 3E pay for itself over time? The walls alone cost about $2,600 a bed more up front and pay that back in about year 28 (worth about $1,300 a bed over 50 years at 5.15%). The whole System 3E building with concrete floors costs about $7,800 a bed more and doesn't pay back within 50 years on upkeep alone; the concrete floors buy fire separation, quiet and a wood-free building. More than half the yearly saving is insurance, so an RIT insurance quote by building type matters, and so does a real System 3E price: each $1 a sq ft of wall moves the answer about $660 a bed. If the first US crews stack no faster than ordinary block, System 3E walls run about $13,800 a bed behind.

Material list v2 counts every material the design needs, nothing more and nothing less: about $162 a sq ft to build and $320 all in, $130,049 a bed, from national installed list prices. The model keeps the more cautious $294 until a Rochester contractor prices it.

## What we propose now

| Item | Current plan |
| --- | --- |
| New beds | 744, sized from RIT's own enrollment and housing data (sourced range 444 to 871) |
| Homes | 18 identical rows of 10 townhomes, private bedrooms, one repeated design, built in System 3E perlite blocks on a concrete slab with vinyl plank inside and timber upper floors |
| Accessible beds | 72 single-level ground-floor beds, against about 38 required |
| Phasing | Design and permits first, a 72-bed pilot in fall 2029, then 4 rows a year, last homes 2033 |
| Total cost | About $170M, or $228k a bed (System 3E stacked at the maker's speed, timber upper floors); about $168M in wood frame |
| Old buildings | Assumed cleared by RIT's own decommissioning; our estimate of that is about $22.6M, shown separately and not in the total |

The bed count comes first and the design meets it. 744 beds would house about 53% of RIT undergraduates on campus, against a peer median of about 60%.

## Who could pay for it

Today's Riverknoll rent can repay about a fifth to a quarter of the cost, whoever borrows. A nonprofit owner or private developer on an RIT ground lease changes who signs the loan, not the size of the gap.

| Scenario | RIT's share |
| --- | --- |
| RIT borrows (base), Riverknoll-level rent | About $119M |
| Other owners and loan terms, same rent | About $108M to $129M |
| Smaller peer-sized homes | About $92M |
| Plus rent near off-campus levels (about 1.5 times today) | About $57M |
| Plus every gift and grant at its high end | About $29M |

Grants and gifts we found add about $10.7M in the base case and up to $38.6M at best. Low-income housing tax credits and HUD college loans do not apply to student housing. The trade-off is clear: the less RIT pays, the more students pay or the smaller the homes.
