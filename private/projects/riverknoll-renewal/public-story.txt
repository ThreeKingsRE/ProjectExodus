Riverknoll Renewal is a student-led proposal for affordable upperclass housing at RIT. RIT plans to close Riverknoll after the 2026–27 year. This page asks what it would take to rebuild it as affordable, all-electric townhomes, and shows all of our work, every version and every option, so anyone can check it and choose for themselves.

The initiative is independent. RIT has not approved or funded it. Figures are concept research and allowances, not bids. Updated November 27, 2026 (model v24.4 with the approvals timeline, phase by phase).

## Latest version: model v24.4, the approvals path, phase by phase

Why does the Town review housing on RIT's own land? New York gives towns site plan review over all private land, colleges included; only state campuses such as SUNY skip it. RIT went through the same steps for Riverknoll's 2019 renovation.

Approve once, build in phases. State environmental rules (6 NYCRR 617.3(g)) treat every phase as one project; reviewing only the pilot is "segmentation", allowed only if the agency shows it is no less protective. So the plan is one environmental review, one site plan with a phasing diagram and one height permit for every 3-story row, then a building permit for each phase. Approving phase by phase would repeat lawyers, hearings and studies four more times (about $0.4M, our rough estimate) and risk a year's slip each round. Henrietta's code lets a site plan lapse if work stalls, so we will ask the Town to confirm in writing that starting the pilot keeps the whole plan valid.

Cost: fees, legal work, hearings and a stormwater guarantee add about $1.5M in today's dollars beyond the design allowance, about $2.1M with contingency, inflation and financing. That makes $172.0M, $231,125 a bed, about $288 a sq ft for the buildings and $570 all in. About $0.9M is a Town surcharge that applies only if the Town counts campus housing as commercial. Missing fall 2029 by a year adds about $7.7M ($10,400 a bed).

## Walls compared three ways: model v24.3, timber upper floors

Asked by the organizer on November 26: a concrete slab with vinyl plank, timber upper floors, and the walls in System 3E, ordinary 2x6 wood framing and factory-built prefab wood panels. Every figure is all in for the same 744 beds; v24.4 adds about $2.1M to each. Timber floors save about $3.9M over the concrete floors of v24 to v24.2.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Total | $169.8M | $180.8M | $168.1M | $169.0M |
| Per bed | $228,266 | $243,017 | $225,948 | $227,183 |
| Per sq ft, buildings / all in | $288 / $562 | $307 / $599 | $285 / $557 | $286 / $560 |
| Outside walls per sq ft of wall | $20.50 | $35.22 | $24.25 | $25.24 |
| Walls alone, per bed | $8,527 | $14,652 | $10,087 | $10,499 |
| Pays back on upkeep | About year 23, only with cheaper insurance | Not within 50 years | Base | No yearly saving |
| 50-year total per bed (5.15%) | $224,330 | $239,082 | $225,948 | $227,183 |

The straight answer: System 3E walls win only if blocks come from a US plant near our assumed $12 a sq ft (not a quote; no US plant is open yet). From Poland they cost about $4,600 a bed more than wood. Even at the US price the whole building costs about $2,300 a bed more, because block inner walls cost more. Over half the yearly saving is cheaper insurance, which RIT has not confirmed. System 3E meets NY code with no added insulation and can't rot, but has one maker, freight and tariff risk, and a European fire test a US official must accept. The line-by-line wall costs are in walls compared v1.

| Old buildings | Cleared by RIT's decommissioning (our estimate about $22.6M, not in the total) |

The bed count comes first and the design meets it: 744 beds would house about 53% of RIT undergraduates on campus (peer median about 60%).

## Zoning: what the Town of Henrietta allows

The Riverknoll area sits in the campus's R-1-15 residential zone. That zone allows colleges but not apartment complexes, so the 18 rows work as RIT's own campus housing. If another group owned or ran them, the site would first need rezoning to R-2-15.

The 2-story rows fit the height rules; the 3-story rows likely top 35 ft and need a Town Board height permit after a hearing, as RIT won in 2022 and 2023. Still to confirm with the Town: that it counts student housing as a college use. Rules are in model v24.4 (Zoning tab).

## History: how Riverknoll got there

RIT decided in 1961 to leave downtown Rochester for about 1,300 acres of Henrietta farmland, broke ground in 1964 and opened the campus in 1968. RIT's archives say Riverknoll (275 apartments and townhouses) and Perkins Green were built then (a 2019 state review says 1970) "because of demand for on campus housing for married students and staff." RIT now says further investment "is neither feasible nor sustainable" and will close it after spring 2027.

Why housing in a zone that bars apartments? Riverknoll predates today's code (1978) and we found no record of its approval. Most likely the Town counts campus housing as part of the college use; an old nonconforming use would lapse a year after closing. Either way, RIT has to own and run the new rows.

## Who could pay for it

Today's Riverknoll rent can repay about a fifth to a quarter of the cost, whoever borrows. A nonprofit owner or private developer on an RIT ground lease changes who signs the loan, not the size of the gap.

| Scenario | RIT's share |
| --- | --- |
| RIT borrows (base), Riverknoll-level rent | About $119M |
| Other owners and loan terms, same rent | About $108M to $129M |
| Smaller peer-sized homes | About $92M |
| Plus rent near off-campus levels (about 1.5 times today) | About $57M |
| Plus every gift and grant at its high end | About $29M |

Gifts and grants we found add about $10.7M, up to $38.6M at best; housing tax credits and HUD college loans don't apply to student housing. The less RIT pays, the more students pay or the smaller the homes.
