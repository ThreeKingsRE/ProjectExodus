Riverknoll Renewal is a student-led proposal for affordable upperclass housing at RIT. RIT plans to close Riverknoll after the 2026–27 year. This page asks what it would take to rebuild it as affordable, all-electric townhomes, and shows all of our work, every version and every option, so anyone can check it and choose for themselves.

The initiative is independent. RIT has not approved or funded it. Figures are concept research and allowances, not bids. Updated November 27, 2026 (model v24.4 with the approvals timeline and cost, walls compared three ways v1, material list v2, deck v28).

## Latest version: model v24.4, the approvals path and its cost

Why does the Town review housing on RIT's own land? New York gives towns zoning and site plan review over all private land, colleges included; courts stop towns from keeping colleges out, not from reviewing their plans. Only state campuses such as SUNY skip it. RIT went through the same steps for Riverknoll's 2019 renovation.

Cost: Town, state and county fees, legal work, hearings and a stormwater guarantee add about $1.5M in today's dollars beyond the design allowance, about $2.1M with contingency, inflation and financing. That makes $172.0M, $231,125 a bed, about $288 a sq ft for the buildings (unchanged) and $570 all in. About $0.9M is a Town surcharge that applies only if the Town counts campus housing as commercial. The bigger risk is delay: missing fall 2029 by a year adds about $7.7M ($10,400 a bed). The tables below are v24.3; v24.4 adds about $2.1M to each.

| Step | Who | Target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Meet RIT housing and facilities; RIT agrees to be owner and applicant | Organizer, RIT | Ask by Dec 11, 2026; letter by Feb 2027 |
| 2. Ask the Town in writing whether campus housing counts as college use | Organizer drafts, RIT signs | Send by Jan 8, 2027 |
| 3. Pre-application meeting with Town planning and engineering | Organizer, RIT | Ask by Feb 26; meet March 2027 |
| 4. RIT trustees approve; design team hired | RIT | Spring to summer 2027 |
| 5. Environmental review and state historic review | Town or DASNY; state | Nov 2027 to Jan 2028 |
| 6. Planning Board site plan; Town Board height permit hearing | Town | Hearings Jan 2028; approval Mar 2028 |
| 7. Stormwater permits; asbestos notices and demolition | RIT, Town, state | Mar to Jun 2028 |
| 8. Pilot building permit, then build | Town, RIT | Permit Jul 2028; open fall 2029 |

About one month of slack. A full environmental impact statement, or RIT signing on after summer 2027, moves the pilot to fall 2030. All 15 steps, with what to bring and the rules, are in model v24.4 (Approvals_Timeline tab).

The straight answer: System 3E walls win only if blocks come from a US plant near our assumed $12 a sq ft (not a quote; no US plant is open yet). Shipped from Poland today they cost about $4,600 a bed more than wood with no payback in 50 years. Even at the US price the whole building costs about $2,300 a bed more than wood, because block inner walls cost more and wood's advanced-framing saving is lost. Over half the yearly saving is cheaper insurance, which RIT has not confirmed.

System 3E meets NY code with no added insulation and can't rot, but has one maker, freight and tariff risk, and a European fire test a US official must accept. 2x6 wood is the cheapest proven way. Prefab panels cut framing days about 39% but cost more and need a crane.

These replace life cycle v2, which assumed concrete floors. Sources are in the research library.

## What we propose now

| Item | Current plan |
| --- | --- |
| New beds | 744, sized from RIT's own enrollment and housing data (sourced range 444 to 871) |
| Homes | 18 rows of 10 townhomes, private bedrooms, System 3E perlite blocks on a slab, timber upper floors |
| Accessible beds | 72 single-level ground-floor beds, against about 38 required |
| Phasing | Design and permits first, a 72-bed pilot in fall 2029, then 4 rows a year, last homes 2033 |
| Total cost | About $172M, or $231k a bed with approvals (v24.4); about $170M in wood frame |
| Old buildings | Cleared by RIT's decommissioning (our estimate about $22.6M, not in the total) |

The bed count comes first and the design meets it. 744 beds would house about 53% of RIT undergraduates on campus, against a peer median of about 60%.

## Zoning: what the Town of Henrietta allows

Checked November 27, 2026 against the Town's zoning map and code. The Riverknoll area sits in the campus's R-1-15 residential zone. That zone allows colleges but not apartment complexes, so the 18 rows work as RIT's own campus housing. If another group owned or ran them, the site would first need rezoning to R-2-15.

The 2-story rows fit the height rules. The 3-story rows would likely top the 35 ft limit and need a Town Board height permit after a public hearing; RIT won two such permits for campus buildings in 2022 and 2023. Still to confirm with the Town: that it counts student housing as a college use. Rules are in model v24.4 (Zoning tab).

## History: how Riverknoll got there

RIT decided in 1961 to leave downtown Rochester for about 1,300 acres of Henrietta farmland, broke ground in 1964 and opened the campus in 1968. RIT's archives say Riverknoll (275 apartments and townhouses) and Perkins Green were built then (a 2019 state review says 1970) "because of demand for on campus housing for married students and staff." Over time it became mostly upperclass student housing. RIT now says further investment "is neither feasible nor sustainable" and will close it after spring 2027.

Why is there housing in a zone that bars apartments? Riverknoll predates today's code (1978) and we found no record of its approval. Most likely the Town counts RIT's campus housing as part of the college use. If it were instead an old nonconforming use, that right ends a year after the use stops. Either way, RIT has to own and run the new rows.

## Who could pay for it

Today's Riverknoll rent can repay about a fifth to a quarter of the cost, whoever borrows. A nonprofit owner or private developer on an RIT ground lease changes who signs the loan, not the size of the gap.

| Scenario | RIT's share |
| --- | --- |
| RIT borrows (base), Riverknoll-level rent | About $119M |
| Other owners and loan terms, same rent | About $108M to $129M |
| Smaller peer-sized homes | About $92M |
| Plus rent near off-campus levels (about 1.5 times today) | About $57M |
| Plus every gift and grant at its high end | About $29M |

Grants and gifts we found add about $10.7M in the base case and up to $38.6M at best. Low-income housing tax credits and HUD college loans do not apply to student housing. The trade-off is clear: the less RIT pays, the more students pay or the smaller the homes.
